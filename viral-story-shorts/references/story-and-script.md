# Story and script system

## The three viewer decisions

1. **Stop:** recognition + tension + destination are clear in the first frame/line.
2. **Stay:** every beat changes the situation or moves toward proof.
3. **Act:** the payoff creates emotion, social value, identity, or genuine conversation.

## Score the idea before scripting

Use the 0–10 STORY test (0–2 each):

- **Stakes:** loss, rejection, pressure, conflict, failure, or identity crisis.
- **Turn:** a precise decision, chance, game, performance, episode, person, or realization.
- **Outcome:** a visible, meaningful contrast from the beginning.
- **Recognition:** famous subject or universal conflict understandable to a cold viewer.
- **Yield of proof:** trustworthy facts, scenes, interviews, and usable visual options.

Prefer 8–10 point stories. A famous name without a clean transformation is usually weaker than a less famous person with universal stakes and proof.

## Default arc

### Promise

The hook should reveal enough premise to create a specific unanswered question. Pair two different channels:

- Visual: recognizable face/moment, motion, outcome preview, or contradiction.
- Voice/text: cost, reversal, stakes, or destination.

Strong patterns:

- Known ending, unknown cost: “You know the Oscar. You don't know the 20 years he spent thinking acting was over.”
- Public verdict versus proof: “They called her body the problem. Then she made joy the thing gymnastics remembered.”
- Identity contradiction: “Richie was the loudest man in the room—until silence taught him what he was good at.”
- Moment before the moment: “This celebration only makes sense if you know what happened eight years earlier.”
- Specific reversal: “At 46, after decades away from acting, he got one final audition.”

Avoid greetings, “today we're talking about,” generic superlatives, unclear pronouns, and naked “you won't believe” withholding.

### Pressure

Choose two or three consequences, not a date list. Answer:

- What did they lose?
- What did the public/industry/team believe?
- What did the subject say the experience did to them?
- What made the next chance difficult?

Compress only after confirming the compression remains accurate.

### Pivot

Name the decision or turning point. “Things got better” is not a pivot. The pivot is where agency, opportunity, and risk meet.

### Proof

Show the routine, score, speech, award, return, changed action, service, embrace, review, or later callback. Let authentic sound or the face breathe briefly when the visual proves the line.

### Meaning

Add one concise interpretation that changes how the viewer understands the famous moment. Avoid generic inspiration language that could close any story.

### Next

Use at most one CTA after the emotional resolution:

- identity-aligned follow promise;
- real interpretive question;
- send to someone for whom the story has meaning;
- standalone next episode tease.

## Causal writing and re-hooks

Replace “and then” chronology with conflict and consequence:

> They offered him a second chance, **but** he no longer trusted the industry. **So** he prepared as if it were his last audition. **That meant** the final performance carried decades of absence.

A re-hook is useful when it advances the question:

- “But getting invited back was not the comeback.”
- “The real change was smaller—and the next episode proved it.”
- “Then the one person he expected to doubt him did the opposite.”

Do not insert empty re-hooks on a timer.

## Word and timing budgets

Start with a natural, emotionally legible pace around 145–165 spoken words per minute, then account for interview inserts, pauses, and names.

| Target | Voiceover starting budget |
|---|---:|
| 20–30 sec | 55–80 words |
| 35–50 sec | 90–130 words |
| 50–75 sec | 125–185 words |

Read the script aloud or synthesize a draft when possible. Do not solve an overlong script by forcing an unnatural voice speed. Cut setup, duplicate meaning, weak adjectives, and unnecessary dates first.

## Line-level rules

- Prefer concrete nouns and active verbs.
- Use short sentences but vary rhythm; fragments are allowed when intentional.
- Explain the minimum context a general viewer needs.
- Name the subject early.
- Avoid stacked subordinate clauses, empty intensifiers, and clichés.
- Use exact numbers/dates only when they sharpen stakes or prevent confusion.
- Do not repeat in narration what the interview clip already says.
- Keep the pasteable voiceover free of production directions.

## Ending tests

The final line should pass at least one:

- **Reframe:** changes the meaning of the opening image.
- **Emotional release:** moves sadness/pressure into awe, relief, admiration, or earned ambiguity.
- **Callback:** repeats an early phrase with new meaning.
- **Conversation:** opens a defensible interpretation after resolving the factual promise.
- **Loop:** returns naturally to the opening without weakening closure.

If the ending could be pasted onto five unrelated stories, rewrite it.

## Hook review

Before delivery, verify:

- A viewer knows the subject or human conflict immediately.
- The first line and visual add different information.
- The question is specific, not confusing.
- The body begins before the hook finishes repeating itself.
- The ending fully satisfies what was promised.
- The hook remains accurate after fact-checking.

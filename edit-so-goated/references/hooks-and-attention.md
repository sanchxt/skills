# Hooks and attention

Use this reference for both voiceover and music-led edits.

## A hook is a contract

The opening must make the next second feel worth more than the next swipe. It needs enough context to be understood and enough missing information, contrast, or emotion to create forward pull. The ending must repay the same promise.

Build the first moment from three ingredients:

- **Recognition:** a known face, costume, name, moment, or universal situation.
- **Tension:** a truthful contradiction, reversal, cost, threat, comparison, or unresolved action.
- **Destination:** the viewer can sense what answer, transformation, reveal, or emotional release is coming.

The first visual and text/voice should add different information rather than duplicating one another.

## Hook families

Choose the family that the evidence and footage can actually pay off.

- **Known result, hidden cost:** show the trophy/scene first; reveal what made it unlikely.
- **Identity contradiction:** “The loudest man in the room learned power from silence.”
- **Public verdict versus proof:** show the criticism, then the undeniable later moment.
- **Moment before the moment:** open on the famous result and point to the earlier cause.
- **Specific reversal:** rejected → needed, defeated → champion, comic relief → emotional center.
- **Impossible constraint:** name the narrow limit the subject overcame without inflating it.
- **Visual transformation:** before/after images share framing, gesture, costume, or location.
- **Authentic sound cold open:** a verified line, crowd sound, impact, breath, or silence starts the question.
- **Outcome-first action:** begin mid-motion or on the strongest image, then rebuild the meaning.
- **Audience interpretation:** a defensible claim that fans can agree or argue with after seeing the proof.

Avoid greetings, logos, black frames, vague pronouns, generic superlatives, “wait for it,” “you won’t believe,” and questions whose only function is to hide the subject.

## Contradiction hooks

Contradiction is useful when it creates **moderate, relevant incongruity**: two ideas that seem incompatible but become coherent through the video. It fails when the mismatch is random, false, too abstract, or requires so much context that a cold viewer cannot frame the question.

Use this test:

1. Can the viewer name both sides of the contrast immediately?
2. Are both sides true or honestly framed?
3. Does the visual prove one side while text/voice introduces the other?
4. Is there one clear question connecting them?
5. Does the video resolve the tension without changing the claim?

Prefer a concrete semantic collision—dangerous/harmless, villain/savior, failure/foundation, silence/power—over empty shock. Curiosity research indicates that withholding everything can backfire; give enough concrete information to make the gap feel solvable and worthwhile.

## Voiceover hook construction

Draft the final payoff line before settling the hook. Keep the spoken opening compact enough to understand on first hearing. Name the subject early unless the image makes identity unmistakable. Use one clean sentence or two short clauses.

For each hook deliver:

- spoken line;
- first-frame visual;
- optional 3–8 word overlay;
- the precise question it opens;
- the exact payoff it promises.

## Music-led hook construction

A no-voice edit still needs a premise. Package the hook as:

- an iconic or high-motion first image;
- optional 3–8 word text, or a justified text-free opening;
- immediate sound texture, recognizable lyric fragment, impact, breath, or beat;
- a visual discrepancy, unfinished action, comparison, or promised transformation;
- a first cut that confirms the edit has direction rather than being a random montage.

Text-free openings should make the relation legible through framing, match cuts, costume/state contrast, or recognizable source dialogue. If the idea is incomprehensible on mute, add minimal text.

## Hold attention without noise

- Advance through cause/consequence in stories and through visible contrast/energy progression in music-led edits.
- Introduce a new piece of meaning, proof, movement, scale, source audio, or musical phrase before the current beat exhausts itself.
- Use pattern changes at semantic or musical boundaries; do not flash captions or effects merely to create activity.
- Alternate density and breathing room. A held face, silence, or natural sound can increase impact after rapid motion.
- Re-hook only after partially answering the previous question.
- Show proof as early as the promise allows. Do not hold one answer hostage for the whole runtime.
- Design for comprehension on a phone: one dominant focal point and one readable text idea at a time.
- Use callbacks, mirrored shots, or a natural loop when they deepen meaning; do not weaken closure for a forced loop.

## Turn attention into action

- **Share/send:** give viewers awe, hidden context, identity, recognition, an emotional gift, or a conversation they want with someone specific.
- **Comment:** ask for interpretation, ranking, alternate turning point, or next subject only when more than one answer is defensible.
- **Follow:** make the next value predictable through a clear channel promise or recurring series.
- **Like:** earn the emotional reaction before asking for it.

Use at most one in-video CTA. For aesthetic edits, a caption or pinned-comment prompt usually preserves the ending better. Never create a deliberate error, false binary, or humiliating bait solely for comments.

## Review before delivery

- A cold viewer can identify the subject or universal conflict immediately.
- The visual and language do complementary work.
- The curiosity gap is specific and proportionate.
- The body begins moving before the hook repeats itself.
- The strongest visual is not buried behind unnecessary setup.
- The promised emotion or answer arrives and gets enough time to register.
- The ending creates satisfaction, share value, conversation, or a clear reason to return.

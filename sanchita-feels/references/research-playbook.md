# Research playbook

Use for trend scans, song suggestions, competitor updates, platform changes, and factual psychology or internet-theory claims.

## Freshness and evidence

Browse whenever a claim could have changed, including account activity, follower counts, audio trends, hashtags, platform features, recommendation rules, and content-policy changes. Record the date checked. Prefer this source order:

1. Platform or creator's live page and official platform documentation
2. Primary research, professional bodies, government or public-health guidance
3. Direct creator interviews and creator-owned sites
4. Reputable journalism
5. Transparent analytics providers
6. Community discussion only as anecdotal discovery, never proof

Use search-result counts and third-party dashboards as directional unless methodology is clear. If Instagram blocks unauthenticated viewing, say what could and could not be verified; do not infer recent performance from follower size.

## Trend scan

For a trend request:

1. Establish platform, audience, region/language if known, and a short recency window appropriate to the trend.
2. Inspect current Reels/Shorts surfaces, creator posts, platform trend tools, and reputable coverage. Search multiple phrasings.
3. Capture the original or earliest traceable usage when possible, recent examples, posting dates, view/engagement signals, saturation, and whether the format is still accelerating.
4. Classify: emerging, rising, active, saturated, declining, or unverifiable.
5. Test niche fit and safety. A popular sound is not automatically useful for mental-health content.
6. Return an original SanchitaFeels adaptation, not a copy; include a rights-safe fallback.

Distinguish trend type: audio, editing grammar, meme/template, topic/search demand, seasonal moment, or cultural conversation. Never label evergreen search demand as a "trend" without evidence of recent acceleration.

## Song search

Check both Instagram and YouTube because audio licensing and availability differ. Prefer native audio-library links or exact searchable names. Report observation date and territory limitation. A track showing an upward indicator or many recent niche uses is evidence; old total-use counts alone do not prove present momentum.

## Competitor review

Analyze the most recent 20–30 relevant posts when access permits, plus the top recent performers. Capture:

- exact handle, platform, credential/positioning, and activity date;
- recurring topics, formats, lengths, first-frame types, hook syntax, visual identity, voice, CTAs, and posting cadence;
- median-like performance rather than only the biggest outlier;
- unusually strong posts normalized against the creator's baseline;
- comment language: what viewers repeat, ask, challenge, or send to others;
- conversion paths: profile promise, pinned content, newsletter, book, community, or longer video;
- what appears to work, what appears stale or weak, and evidence quality.

Clearly label causal limits: public observation can show correlation and patterns, not why the algorithm distributed a post. Do not copy private data, scrape in violation of access controls, or present estimated revenue as fact.

## Psychology claim check

Turn the intended statement into a narrow claim. Search scholarly databases, systematic reviews, recognized professional bodies, and authoritative health sources. Record:

- what the source actually studied;
- population and context;
- whether the result is correlational, experimental, clinical guidance, or expert interpretation;
- limits and reasonable plain-language wording.

Avoid "psychology says" and "studies prove." Prefer "research suggests," followed by the real scope. Do not cite a broad brain concept to justify a specific relationship conclusion.

Useful starting points:

- APA on mental-health misinformation: <https://www.apa.org/topics/journalism-facts/misinformation-mental-health>
- APA consensus statement on health misinformation: <https://www.apa.org/pubs/reports/health-misinformation>
- WHO guidance for online mental-health communication with young people: <https://www.who.int/publications/m/item/report-of-a-virtual-roundtable-meeting-on-online-mental-health-content-for-young-people-and-guidance-on-communication--4-october-2023>
- YouTube on appeal, engagement, satisfaction, and hooks: <https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/16559650>
- Instagram Trial Reels: <https://about.fb.com/news/2024/12/trial-reels-try-content-non-followers-first-see-what-perfoms-best/>

## Theory taxonomy

Before scripting a named "theory," classify it:

- **Established scientific theory/model:** supported and defined in a scientific field.
- **Research concept or effect:** narrower empirical construct; explain limits.
- **Therapeutic framework:** a clinical model, not a universal fact.
- **Philosophical/spiritual belief or cultural tradition:** describe respectfully and avoid scientific claims.
- **Internet metaphor/trend:** useful for meaning-making, not evidence.
- **Unverified or misattributed claim:** correct it or decline to amplify it.

For invisible-string, red-string, or burnt-toast content, research the precise version and origins. Unless strong evidence shows otherwise, frame these as cultural stories, spiritual beliefs, or internet metaphors—not clinical psychology.

## Research output

Return:

- dated executive takeaway;
- evidence table with claim, source, date, strength, and limitation;
- creative implications for this account;
- what to test now;
- what remains unverified.

Link directly to sources. Never fabricate a source to make a hook sound authoritative.
